# 1 · What the company is

**One platform, one credential layer, one property record — sold to five different customers.**

Every product line below runs on the same spine: a verified professional, a documented property, an AI-triaged work item, a compliant dispatch, a payment, and a permanent record. What changes per line is who the customer is and what the work item is called.

| Line | Customer | Work item | Model |
|---|---|---|---|
| **ProLnk** | Service professionals | Job | Subscription + platform fee |
| **TrustyPro** | Homeowners | Service request | Marketplace + commerce |
| **TrustyPro Renters** | Residents (via operators) | Maintenance request | Free to resident; monetized around it |
| **TrustyPro Portfolio** | Rental operators (AMH) | Approval / work order | Free license + revenue share |
| **TrustyPro Exchange** | Commercial contractors (CoolSys) | Work order | White-label license + fees |

**The compounding asset underneath all five: the credential layer and the property record.** A pro verified once is verified everywhere. A home documented once is documented forever. Neither is copyable by a competitor without redoing the work.

# 5 · The three-engine strategy

**Engine 1 — Enterprise (Portfolio + Exchange). Funds everything.**
Few customers, high value, fast cash, 2–4 quarter cycles. By Y5 this is $27M of high-margin revenue. Its real job is earlier: it pays for the build while the consumer side is still small, and it delivers *supply and demand in blocks* — one operator signature is 60,000 homes; one commercial signature can credential thousands of contractors.

**Engine 2 — Consumer marketplace (TrustyPro + ProLnk). The volume.**
Low ARPU, high count, slow to build, largest at scale. $62M by Y5 and the overwhelming majority of the Y10–Y15 picture. Enterprise channels are what make it grow without consumer marketing spend.

**Engine 3 — Data. Zero today, terminal value tomorrow.**
$3.8M by Y5 at Tier 1 pricing only. The value is in what it does to the multiple: at Tier 3 (risk participation), documented homes are worth $75–300 each per year. This engine is entirely dependent on the first two producing consented, verified, seasoned records — which is why the consent chain and provenance tagging are Day-1 requirements, not later features.

**The order matters: enterprise buys time, consumer builds the asset, data prices the company.**

| Vendor subscription adoption (commercial) | 40% | 20% | Y5 commercial $19.2M → ~$13M |

| Affiliate commerce per home | $60 owner / $12 rental | Half | Y5 total −$4.8M |
| Data multiple realized | 8–10× | 5–6× | Y5 valuation $710M → $440M |
| Pro network growth | 15,000 by Y5 | 8,000 | Y5 total −$13M and constrains job fulfillment |

**The single largest swing is homeowner engagement.** Rental doors arrive in blocks and are predictable; homeowners must be won and retained. Every channel strategy — builders, warranty, inspectors, HOAs, renters-become-owners — exists to de-risk that one line.

**Y1 is near-breakeven and Y2 is profitable in this model** — meaning the raise buys speed, not survival. That is the strongest possible negotiating position.

# 8 · Risks

- **Execution capacity.** The 120-day launch is the gate on everything. Every projection above assumes it lands.
- **Commercial focus split.** Selling Exchange before the residential launch is the highest-probability self-inflicted wound. Sell now, build after Day 120.
- **Consent chain.** No consent chain, no data business — and the data business is most of the terminal value.
- **Platform forking.** Five product lines on one codebase is the plan; five codebases is the failure mode.
- **Single-anchor concentration.** AMH is ~40% of near-term rental revenue. Operator #2 matters more than it looks.
- **Payout and photo integrity.** A commission bug or lost Move-In Shield photos at pilot scale would end operator credibility permanently.
